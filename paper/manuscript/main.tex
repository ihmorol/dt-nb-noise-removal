\begin{abstract}
We evaluate how supervised filtering order and the placement of cleaning
relative to cross-validation affect decision tree--Na\"ive Bayes hybrids.
The studied steps remove training observations misclassified by a
Na\"ive Bayes judge and select attributes using a decision tree, with
inverse-square-root depth weights for Na\"ive Bayes. We compose these steps
in both orders and compare them with uncleaned classifiers, single-step
methods, and an independent parallel composition. The evaluation uses
Weka C4.5 and a mixed nominal/numeric Na\"ive Bayes implementation on ten
benchmark datasets, with ten repetitions of stratified 10-fold
cross-validation. Under training-fold-specific cleaning, the two combined
tree methods average 81.21\% and 80.89\% accuracy, compared with 86.33\% for
uncleaned C4.5. The combined Na\"ive Bayes methods average 75.63\% and
76.35\%, compared with 75.93\% without cleaning. Cleaning the complete
dataset before cross-validation raises the combined tree scores to
94.72\% and 96.32\%, but also changes the population being evaluated.
These differences therefore do not isolate leakage bias. Exploratory
dataset-level tests provide no significant comparison after correction
across 24 hypotheses. Removal records reveal class-dependent deletion and
changes in tree-selected attributes after instance filtering. The findings
support explicit evaluation and removal diagnostics for these hybrids;
they do not establish improved classification or verified noise detection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Supervised cleaning can change both the observations available to a
classifier and the attributes it uses. Farid et al.~\cite{farid2014hybrid}
introduced two independent decision tree (DT)--Na\"ive Bayes (NB) hybrids:
an NB judge removes training observations before a tree is grown, and a
tree selects and weights attributes before NB is fitted. These steps offer
a direct setting in which to examine whether composing two cleaners adds
value beyond their individual use.

The order of the steps changes their inputs. After instance removal, a
tree selects attributes from a different training sample. After attribute
selection, NB judges observations through a different representation.
However, neither composition nor a smaller training set guarantees a
better classifier. Earlier work on joint instance and feature selection
already examined ordering~\cite{tsai2013genetic}, while research on
misclassification-based filters identified the risk of rejecting valid
exceptions~\cite{brodley1999identifying,john1995robust}.

Evaluation introduces a separate issue. A supervised cleaner fitted before
cross-validation can use information from future held-out observations.
This is related to established concerns about feature-selection leakage
and performance evaluation~\cite{demircioglu2021measuring,cawley2010overfitting,
kapoor2023leakage}. Instance removal also changes the evaluation population
if rejected rows disappear before folds are created. A higher score on
those surviving rows answers a different question from a score on every
held-out observation.

We investigate three questions. Does either composition improve on its
matched uncleaned classifier or single-step alternatives? Does changing
order alter classification and removal behavior? How do scores change
when cleaning is performed before, rather than within, cross-validation?
The last comparison is a protocol sensitivity analysis; it does not
identify which implementation was used in the original study.

We implement the two steps on the original attribute columns with Weka
J48 and a mixed-likelihood NB model. All main comparisons share training
and test splits. Single-step and parallel reference methods help separate
composition from the effects already present in individual steps. The
main protocol refits supervised cleaning inside each training fold and
scores every test row.

The study contributes an implementation-explicit comparison of the two
orders, a descriptive contrast between two evaluation protocols, and
fold-level removal diagnostics. Its contribution is empirical analysis
of these particular hybrids, rather than a new tree-weighting rule or a
claim that filtering generally fails. The tested combined methods do not
show a consistent advantage over their uncleaned counterparts.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Combining instance and attribute operations}
Farid et al.~\cite{farid2014hybrid} used NB filtering for a tree and
tree-derived weights for NB as separate algorithms. Hall~\cite{hall2007decision}
also derived NB attribute weights from minimum tree depth, but stabilized
them through bagged unpruned trees. Thus the weighting principle is prior
work; our implementation uses one pruned tree, following the studied
single-tree design. Tsai et al.~\cite{tsai2013genetic} investigated feature
and instance selection priorities using genetic algorithms with other
classifiers. Their study motivates an ordering comparison, but does not
supply results for the specific NB/tree steps tested here. We make no
claim to be the first study of preprocessing order.

\subsection{Disagreement is not a verified label error}
Brodley and Friedl~\cite{brodley1999identifying} used classifier-based
filtering and examined the tradeoff between rejecting mislabeled and
correctly labeled observations. Their filters obtained predictions through
internal cross-validation, whereas our studied judge uses its training
predictions. John~\cite{john1995robust} iteratively removed errors of a
pruned tree and discussed potential losses on valid, underrepresented
patterns. These findings motivate a distinction between classifier
disagreement and known corruption. More recent work on confident
learning~\cite{northcutt2021confident} and the comparison of correction
with filtering~\cite{jiang2024correction} addresses label uncertainty
through different procedures. Those methods are context, not evaluated
competitors in this study.

\subsection{Evaluation and statistical comparison}
Cawley and Talbot~\cite{cawley2010overfitting} analyzed selection bias in
performance evaluation, while Demircio\u{g}lu~\cite{demircioglu2021measuring}
examined feature selection before versus within cross-validation.
Kapoor and Narayanan~\cite{kapoor2023leakage} situated leakage within
broader reproducibility problems. We apply their methodological concern
to supervised DT--NB cleaning, while explicitly recognizing that row
filtering changes the cases subsequently scored. For statistical
comparisons, we use dataset-level summaries following
Dem\v{s}ar~\cite{demsar2006statistical}; repetitions of overlapping folds
are not treated as additional independent datasets.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Base classifiers and notation}
Let $D$ contain labeled training observations and let $S$ denote the
selected attributes. DT is Weka 3.8.6 J48 (C4.5), with pruning enabled,
confidence factor 0.25 and minimum leaf size 2~\cite{witten2016weka}.
NB uses empirical class priors, add-one smoothing for nominal attributes,
and a class-conditional Gaussian likelihood for numeric attributes.
Numeric population variances receive $10^{-9}$ times the largest numeric
attribute variance in the current training sample. The implementation
scores a class $c$ by
\begin{equation}
\log P(c)+\sum_{j\in S}w_j\log P(x_j\mid c),
\label{eq:nb}
\end{equation}
and predicts the class with the greatest score. Plain NB sets $w_j=1$.
A class absent from NB's training sample cannot be predicted by that fit.

\subsection{Algorithm 1: instance filtering (N)}
The NB judge is fitted to its supplied training sample and predicts those
same rows. Rows whose predictions disagree with their labels are removed.
This is in-sample disagreement filtering, not out-of-fold label-error
estimation. If removal would leave fewer than two classes, the operation
is skipped and all supplied rows are retained. The safeguard does not
preserve every original class in a multiclass dataset. We call discarded
rows \emph{removed observations}, since no independent corruption labels
are available.

The original single-step arrangement ends with DT and is denoted N--DT.
N--NB is a reference using the same removal decision with NB as the final
classifier. Neither method filters held-out rows under the main protocol.

\subsection{Algorithm 2: attribute selection and weighting (A)}
A tree is grown on the supplied training rows. Attribute $j$ receives
$w_j=1/\sqrt{d_j}$, where $d_j$ is the smallest depth at which that tree
tests it and root depth is one. Attributes not tested are omitted.
A--NB uses these weights in Eq.~\ref{eq:nb}; A--DT refits a tree on the
retained attributes without using NB weights. Selection is based on
original attributes, not separate one-hot indicators.

An empty-selection safeguard matters for reproducibility. In the main
fold-specific implementation, an empty tree selection retains all
attributes at unit weights, including for A--NB. In the before-CV
implementation, standalone A--NB instead uses a priors-only model when
selection is empty; composed paths retain all attributes. No empty-selection
or class-collapse safeguard was triggered in the saved main-protocol
records, so the empty-tree difference was not exercised there.

\subsection{Algorithm 3: ordered and parallel compositions}
NA applies N using plain NB and then fits A on the surviving rows. AN
fits A first and passes its selected attributes and weights to the NB
judge in N. The latter weights also enter the final NB. Either order
ends with NB or DT, giving four combined methods (Fig.~\ref{fig:algorithm}).

A parallel reference, P, fits plain-NB filtering and tree attribute
selection independently on the same original training fold. The final
classifier uses the intersection of retained rows and retained attributes;
final NB uses the tree's weights. Comparing P with NA or AN measures the
consequence of sequential dependence under these implementation choices.
It does not isolate ordering from judge weighting: AN also changes the
representation and weights used by its judge.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\begin{minipage}{0.98\columnwidth}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Training data $D$; order $o$; final classifier $f$
\Ensure Fitted model $M$ and selected attributes $S$
\State $S\gets$ all attributes; $W\gets$ unit weights
\If{$o=\mathrm{AN}$}
  \State Fit A on $D$; obtain $S,W$
  \State If $S$ is empty, keep all attributes at unit weights
  \State Fit weighted NB on $D[:,S]$; identify disagreements
  \State Remove disagreements if at least two classes remain
\Else
  \State Fit plain NB on $D$; identify disagreements
  \State Remove disagreements if at least two classes remain
  \State Fit A on surviving $D$; obtain $S,W$
  \State If $S$ is empty, keep all attributes at unit weights
\EndIf
\If{$f=\mathrm{NB}$}
  \State Fit NB on surviving $D[:,S]$ with $W$
\Else
  \State Fit J48 on surviving $D[:,S]$
\EndIf
\State \Return $M,S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{minipage}

NA means instances first; AN means attributes first. Both safeguards are
implementation choices. At prediction time, select $S$ on every held-out
row and apply $M$; do not remove held-out rows.}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Datasets and preprocessing}
We use the ten dataset names considered by Farid et al., with the locally
available files summarized in Table~\ref{tab:data}. Iris is loaded through
scikit-learn; NSL-KDD uses its 20\% training subset~\cite{tavallaee2009detailed}.
Image segmentation combines the two available UCI files. Consequently its
2,310 rows differ from the 1,500 reported in the original study. Glass has
six observed classes rather than seven; NSL-KDD has 22 rather than 23.
These are implementation-specific benchmark experiments, not an exact
reproduction of the original unpublished implementation.

Original nominal attributes remain single columns. Row identifiers and
NSL-KDD's difficulty field are excluded. Literal question-mark values are
treated as nominal categories, without imputation. No numeric scaling is
applied. Nominal vocabularies are constructed once from the complete files
before splitting. They do not use labels, but influence NB smoothing
denominators. Thus the main experiment is fold-local in supervised fitting,
with a shared, label-free attribute schema; it is not a completely
inductive preprocessing pipeline.

\subsection{Two evaluation protocols}
Protocol B, the main evaluation, uses ten repetitions of shuffled,
stratified 10-fold cross-validation with seeds 0--9. Within a split, all
12 methods start with identical training and test rows. Every supervised
cleaning step and final classifier is refitted using training rows only.
A test fold loses the columns excluded by its training-fold selector but
retains all its rows. Each method has 100 fold scores per dataset.

Protocol A fits the supervised cleaners once on the complete dataset,
then cross-validates only the final classifier on the resulting rows and
attributes using the same seed range. Filtering can use labels of future
test cases and exclude those cases from evaluation altogether. Its
accuracy therefore describes selected survivors, not held-out performance
on the original population. Protocol-A folds are not paired with B folds.
The A-minus-B difference combines information access, sample selection,
and changes in fitted models; it is not an isolated causal estimate of
leakage bias. We do not infer that the original study used A.

Ten folds are retained even when classes have fewer than ten observations.
Contact lenses has class counts 4, 5, and 15, and NSL-KDD includes singleton
classes. Some training folds therefore lack classes found in their test
folds. This limitation is shared by the compared methods and must be
considered when interpreting class-sensitive measures.

\subsection{Measures, aggregation, and exploratory inference}
Accuracy and macro-F1 are calculated for each held-out fold. Macro-F1
averages classes occurring in that fold's true labels or predictions,
assigning zero F1 where necessary; classes absent from both are excluded.
The contributing class set can therefore vary across folds. Dataset
scores are arithmetic means of 100 fold percentages, rather than metrics
computed from pooled predictions. Overall means weight the ten datasets
equally. We report the sample standard deviation of the overall mean
across the ten split seeds as descriptive split sensitivity, not as a
standard error or confidence interval.

For each cleaning event we record rows and attributes before and after
cleaning, plus the removal fraction within each class. Event totals are
counted once per dataset, seed, fold, and stage; per-class records must not
be counted as additional cleaning events.

The statistical analysis was added after inspecting the saved results and
is exploratory. Twelve contrasts cover the original single-step methods
against their matched baselines, four combined methods against baselines,
two order comparisons, and four combined-versus-parallel comparisons.
Each is tested for accuracy and macro-F1, making a family of 24 hypotheses.
Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests use ten paired dataset means, with
zero differences excluded and exhaustive sign permutations to handle
ties. Differences are rounded to ten decimal places before ranking.
Holm correction is applied jointly across the 24 tests at level 0.05.
Neither 100 overlapping folds nor ten repetitions increase the number of
independent datasets~\cite{demsar2006statistical}. Protocol contrasts and
removal diagnostics are descriptive and are not included in this family.

Uncleaned C4.5 achieved 86.33\% mean accuracy and 81.92\% macro-F1.
NA--DT and AN--DT achieved 81.21\% and 80.89\% accuracy, respectively:
differences of $-5.12$ and $-5.44$ percentage points (pp). Their macro-F1
scores were lower by 6.16 and 7.99 pp. A--DT remained close to its baseline
at 86.19\% accuracy, whereas N--DT averaged 81.03\%. Thus adding attribute
selection alone changed the tree mean little; methods involving instance
removal had lower means on this suite.

For NB, A--NB averaged 77.35\% accuracy and 71.73\% macro-F1, compared
with 75.93\% and 69.80\% without cleaning. The combined NA--NB and AN--NB
accuracies were 75.63\% and 76.35\%; their baseline differences were
$-0.30$ and $+0.42$ pp. AN--NB had higher accuracy than NA--NB on eight
of ten datasets, but its mean advantage was only 0.71 pp. The tree order
comparison went in the opposite direction on the overall mean:
AN--DT was 0.32 pp below NA--DT. There is no common winning order across
final classifiers and metrics.

Dataset results expose substantial heterogeneity. On glass, C4.5 scored
66.58\% accuracy, compared with 48.97\% for NA--DT and 49.15\% for AN--DT.
On vote, the corresponding scores were 95.08\%, 95.13\%, and 95.54\%.
For NB on glass, the baseline scored 45.95\%, whereas the combined
methods scored 30.93\% and 37.28\%. These contrasts show why an overall
mean alone is insufficient to characterize the cleaners.

\subsection{Sequential dependence and statistical uncertainty}
P--NB averaged 76.39\% accuracy and 70.47\% macro-F1; P--DT averaged
81.05\% and 75.58\%. The sequential methods did not show a consistent
mean advantage over this independent reference. Table~\ref{tab:tests}
reports both raw and Holm-adjusted $p$-values for the full contrast family.
None met the corrected 0.05 threshold. AN--DT versus C4.5 had raw
accuracy $p=0.014$, but adjusted $p=0.328$. The NB order comparison had
raw $p=0.275$, and the DT order comparison had raw $p=0.922$.
These tests do not establish equality or practical equivalence. With ten
heterogeneous datasets, a nonsignificant result can coexist with meaningful
losses on particular tasks.

\subsection{Before-CV cleaning changes the apparent result}
The protocol contrast in Table~\ref{tab:protocols} was much larger for
methods involving instance removal than for attribute selection alone.
NA--DT increased from 81.21\% in B to 94.72\% in A, and AN--DT from
80.89\% to 96.32\%, differences of 13.51 and 15.43 pp.
N--DT differed by 13.50 pp. In contrast, A--NB differed by 0.72 pp and
A--DT by 0.33 pp. These are observed score differences under different
procedures and, for row-filtering methods, different evaluation samples.
They are not estimates of deployable improvement.

\subsection{What the filters removed}
Table~\ref{tab:removal} summarizes 100 events per stage per dataset.
Plain-NB removal ranged from 4.14\% of training rows on iris to 47.31\%
on glass. The tree retained 97.00\% of glass attributes when fitted on
the original training fold, but 61.00\% after plain-NB filtering. On vote,
these fractions were 27.38\% and 7.62\%. These observations directly show
that the first operation changes the fitted output of the second, even
where classifier scores are close.

Breast-cancer recurrence cases made up 29.72\% of training rows on
average. Plain NB removed 50.63\% of that class versus 14.51\% of
non-recurrence cases. After attribute selection, weighted NB removed
58.97\% and 11.72\%, respectively. In contact lenses, the weighted filter
entirely removed class 1 from 12 of 100 training folds; in glass, it entirely
removed class 3 from nine folds. The two-class safeguard did not prevent
these losses because other classes remained. These are class-removal
diagnostics, not evidence that the removed labels were wrong.

\section{Discussion}
The reference methods narrow the interpretation of composition. Attribute
selection followed by DT produced almost the baseline tree mean, while
adding instance filtering did not recover an overall tree advantage.
For NB, the attribute-only method had a higher mean than either combined
order. Composition therefore cannot be justified from the premise that
both component steps are beneficial independently. Their outputs interact,
but an interaction in retained rows or attributes need not improve
held-out prediction.

The class-removal records are consistent with the concern raised by
Brodley and Friedl~\cite{brodley1999identifying} and
John~\cite{john1995robust}: a classifier can reject difficult but valid
observations. Our results do not establish that mechanism as the cause of
the losses, since we lack verified noise labels and a controlled corruption
intervention. Nor do they contradict benefits reported for different
label-cleaning approaches~\cite{northcutt2021confident,jiang2024correction}.
The warranted conclusion concerns this in-sample NB disagreement rule and
these benchmark conditions.

Protocol A reveals how an apparently strong score can coexist with a
weaker evaluation on all held-out cases. It combines access to future
test information with selection of cases that survive a supervised judge.
The distinction is especially consequential when filtering removes a
whole class. Reporting only the survivor score would conceal that change
in the prediction task. A useful follow-up would keep the original test
population fixed while varying access to training information, separating
selection effects from leakage more directly.

\section{Limitations and Reproducibility}
These ten legacy datasets were chosen for continuity with the seed study,
not as a random sample of classification tasks. Dataset dimensions,
question-mark handling, NB variance stabilization, and tree settings can
differ from the original implementation. We did not reproduce its complete
software or establish its cleaning placement. In particular, the original
study's aggregate claims cannot be attributed to leakage from this audit.

Rare classes and small folds limit macro-F1 interpretation. A variable
fold class set is different from averaging a fixed dataset-wide class
set, and NB cannot predict an absent training class. The shared full-file
nominal vocabulary is another boundary on inductive evaluation. No
controlled label-noise experiment, modern classifier comparison, external
NSL-KDD test-set evaluation, or runtime benchmark is included. The
exploratory tests account for multiple comparisons but do not turn this
selected benchmark suite into a population-level sample.

The reported results derive from saved metrics, per-fold scores, removal
records, and configuration files. The table-generation script recomputes
dataset means, checks them against the stored aggregates, verifies the
expected fold coverage, and derives the statistical comparisons and removal
tables. The numerical audit uses the saved experiments; it does not
constitute a fresh rerun of every model. The analysis can be reproduced
from the repository root by running
\texttt{python paper/manuscript/scripts/}\allowbreak\texttt{make\_results\_table.py}.

\section{Conclusion}
Composing NB disagreement filtering with tree-based attribute selection
changed removal behavior without producing a consistent classification
advantage on the tested suite. The observed order preference depended on
the final classifier, and no exploratory comparison remained significant
after correction. Cleaning before cross-validation yielded much higher
scores for row-filtering methods, while also changing which observations
were evaluated. Together, these findings motivate reporting the cleaning
protocol, matched baselines, and per-class removal records alongside
accuracy. Future work should test the suspected hard-case mechanism with
known corruption labels, preserve rare classes explicitly, and compare
protocols on a fixed held-out population.
