\begin{abstract}
Na\"ive Bayes (NB) can filter training instances before a decision tree is
fitted, while a tree can select and weight attributes for NB. We combine
these steps as Algorithm~3 and test both orders, with either NB or C4.5 as
the final classifier. The evaluation uses ten benchmark datasets and ten
repetitions of stratified 10-fold cross-validation. Every cleaning step is
refitted within the training fold, and every held-out row is scored. Mean
accuracy is 81.21\% and 80.89\% for the two combined tree methods, compared
with 86.33\% for uncleaned C4.5. The combined NB methods reach 75.63\% and
76.35\%, compared with 75.93\% for plain NB and 77.35\% for attribute
weighting alone. Neither order shows a clear accuracy advantage in paired
dataset-level tests. Cleaning before cross-validation raises Algorithm~1's mean tree accuracy
by 13.50 percentage points, but also removes
cases from the evaluation sample. Class-specific removal records show
that the filter can discard a much larger share of one class. On these
datasets, combining the steps does not establish an improvement over the
base classifiers; the protocol and the discarded cases are central to
interpreting the scores.
\end{abstract}

Na\"ive Bayes, decision tree, instance filtering, attribute weighting,

\section{Introduction}
Farid et al.~\cite{farid2014hybrid} introduced two hybrids that connect
Na\"ive Bayes (NB) and decision trees. Algorithm~1 trains NB on the training
set, removes the instances it misclassifies, and grows a tree on the
remaining rows. Algorithm~2 grows a tree first, keeps the attributes it
tests, and gives greater weight to attributes used nearer the root when
fitting NB. Each method lets one classifier prepare data for the other.

Applying both steps is a natural extension, but their combination changes
what each learner sees. If instances are removed first, the tree selects
attributes from a smaller sample. If attributes are selected first, a
weighted NB judge decides which instances remain. We call this combined
procedure Algorithm~3 and examine both orders. A final tree and a final
NB are evaluated separately so that a change of classifier does not stand
in for evidence that cleaning helped.

The evaluation procedure also changes the meaning of a score. A filter
fitted before cross-validation can inspect labels from future test folds
and remove those cases before they are evaluated. Fitting the filter
inside each training fold leaves all held-out rows available for scoring.
The first procedure therefore changes both the information used for
cleaning and the population on which accuracy is measured.

We compare Algorithm~3 with the individual steps and uncleaned baselines
under a shared, fold-specific protocol. The analysis covers accuracy,
macro-F1, and class-specific deletion rates. It also contrasts cleaning
inside the training fold with cleaning before the split. The combined
methods do not show a clear advantage on this benchmark. Our contribution
is the controlled comparison and its explanation through protocol effects
and removal records.

\section{Related work}
Tree-based weighting for NB has an established history. Hall~\cite{hall2007decision}
used decision trees to derive attribute weights, addressing NB's
conditional-independence assumption. Farid et al.~\cite{farid2014hybrid}
proposed the two specific steps studied here and evaluated them separately.
Algorithm~3 composes those steps while retaining their underlying tree
learner and NB likelihood model.

Instance filtering requires a separate caution. Brodley and
Friedl~\cite{brodley1999identifying} studied the identification of mislabeled
training data using several classifiers. A single NB judge's disagreement
with a supplied label cannot establish that the label is wrong. In this
study, ``filtered'' means rejected by that judge, without a claim that the
removed rows contain verified label errors.

Cawley and Talbot~\cite{cawley2010overfitting} showed how evaluation practices
can introduce selection bias large enough to affect comparisons between
learning algorithms. We examine the placement of supervised cleaning for
this particular hybrid family. Dataset-level paired comparisons follow
the framework discussed by Dem\v{s}ar~\cite{demsar2006statistical}; repeated
folds serve to estimate each dataset's score rather than provide independent
observations for the significance tests.

\section{Algorithms}
\subsection{Algorithm 1: instance filtering}
Given training data $D$, the NB judge is fitted on $D$ and predicts the
labels of those same rows. A row remains only when the prediction agrees
with its supplied label. Algorithm~1 then fits C4.5 on the retained rows.
The reference arm ending in NB uses the same filter but refits NB as its
final classifier. If deletion would leave fewer than two classes, our
implementation retains the original training rows.

This is a training-set disagreement rule. Its predictions are not
out-of-fold estimates of label quality. A judge can reject a correctly
labeled case because the class distributions overlap or because its own
model assumptions are unsuitable.

\subsection{Algorithm 2: attribute selection and weighting}
A C4.5 tree is fitted on the training data. Let $d_j$ be the smallest depth
at which it tests attribute $j$, with the root at depth one. The weight is
\begin{equation}
 w_j = \begin{cases}1/\sqrt{d_j}, & j\text{ is tested},\\
                    0, & \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Untested attributes are dropped. The weighted NB class score is
\begin{equation}
 s_c(x)=\log P(c)+\sum_{j\in S}w_j\log P(x_j\mid c),
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the retained attribute set. NB predicts the class with the
largest score. The reference arm ending in a tree uses the selected
columns, but its final C4.5 does not use NB's attribute weights.

\subsection{Algorithm 3: the combined hybrid}
The instances-first path, denoted NA, runs Algorithm~1's plain NB filter
and then derives the selected attributes and weights from a tree on the
retained rows. The attributes-first path, AN, derives the attributes and
weights from the original training fold, then uses weighted NB to filter
its rows. In AN, the same weights reach both the judge and the final NB;
they are not re-estimated after deletion. Each path ends in either weighted
NB or C4.5.

\setcounter{algorithm}{2}
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Combined cleaning and classification}
\label{alg:three}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Training data $D$, order $o\in\{\mathrm{NA},\mathrm{AN}\}$,
 final learner $L\in\{\mathrm{NB},\mathrm{DT}\}$
\If{$o=\mathrm{NA}$}
 \State $D\gets\Call{Filter}{D,\text{plain NB}}$
 \State $(S,W)\gets\Call{Select}{D,\text{C4.5}}$
\Else
 \State $(S,W)\gets\Call{Select}{D,\text{C4.5}}$
 \State $D\gets D[:,S]$
 \State $D\gets\Call{Filter}{D,\text{NB with }W}$
\EndIf
\If{$o=\mathrm{NA}$}
 \State $D\gets D[:,S]$
\EndIf
\State Fit $M$ on $D$ using $L$ (use $W$ if $L=\mathrm{NB}$)
\State \Return $M,S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:three} uses the filtering safeguard stated above. If a
selection tree tests no attribute, the combined path keeps all current
columns with unit weights. No empty-selection event occurs in the stored
runs. At prediction time, the test data keep the selected columns $S$ and
all their rows. Every held-out case must still be classified.

\section{Experimental method}
\subsection{Datasets and implementation}

reports the sizes of our available copies. Image segmentation combines
the two available UCI files, and NSL-KDD uses its 20\% training file
\cite{tavallaee2009detailed}. The available Glass and NSL-KDD files contain
one fewer class than the counts listed in the original study. These
choices allow a comparison within a common benchmark family, but do not
establish an exact reproduction of the original data split.

C4.5 is Weka J48 version 3.8.6 with pruning enabled and defaults
\texttt{-C 0.25 -M 2}~\cite{witten2016weka}. Our NB implementation uses
add-one counts for nominal attributes and Gaussian likelihoods for numeric
attributes. Nominal attributes remain single columns. A tree therefore
selects an original attribute rather than individual one-hot indicators.
Category vocabularies are taken from the available dataset files and held
fixed across folds. Missing nominal values are represented by a separate
category. Counts, numeric distributions, and class priors are estimated
from training rows.

\subsection{Protocols and comparison arms}
Protocol B is the main evaluation. We run stratified 10-fold
cross-validation with seeds 0--9, giving 100 scored folds per dataset and
method. All methods share each split. The judge, selection tree, and final
classifier are fitted within that split's training rows. Only the selected
columns are transferred to the test fold. Test rows are never filtered.
This is repeated cross-validation with fold-specific preprocessing, not
nested hyperparameter search.

Protocol A fits the cleaning steps once on the whole dataset and then
cross-validates the final classifier on the cleaned data. This gives the
cleaner access to future test labels. For instance-filtering methods, it
also removes future test cases and creates folds on a different sample.
We report the resulting score changes descriptively. Protocol A folds are
not paired with Protocol B folds, and their differences cannot isolate
label leakage from evaluation-sample selection.

The comparison includes plain NB and C4.5, the original Algorithm~1--DT
and Algorithm~2--NB methods, and the four versions of Algorithm~3.
Two reference arms complete the attribution: Algorithm~1--NB changes only
the final learner after filtering, and Algorithm~2--DT applies selection
before another tree. N denotes filtering, A denotes attribute selection
and weighting, and NA and AN denote the combined orders throughout the
tables. Older CART experiments and later alternative filtering treatments
are outside this paper's comparison.

\subsection{Measures and paired analysis}
For each method and dataset, accuracy and macro-F1 are averaged over its
100 scored folds. Accuracy gives each fold equal weight. Macro-F1 first
averages class F1 scores over classes present in that fold's true labels
or predictions, assigning zero when a class has no correct prediction.
The reported summary then gives each of the ten datasets equal weight.
It is not a pooled score over all benchmark instances.

We compare eight pairs using two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests on the
ten dataset-level accuracy averages: the two original hybrids against their
respective baselines, each of the four combined methods against its
baseline, and the two order comparisons. Exact permutation enumeration
handles the small sample, including zero differences. Holm adjustment
controls the family of eight comparisons at 0.05. This analysis was added
during manuscript revision using the saved results; it is not a
prospectively registered test family. Mean differences are reported in
percentage points (pp). Comparisons with the single-step methods are
descriptive mean comparisons; the tests cover baselines and order.
A nonsignificant test does not establish equivalence.

For each filtering step, we also record the fraction removed within each
class, using that class's training-row count as the denominator. This
measure shows how deletion changes class representation without assuming
that the discarded labels were incorrect.

\section{Results and discussion}
\subsection{Combined methods have lower mean tree accuracy}
Table~\ref{tab:results} gives the benchmark averages, and
Table~\ref{tab:results-datasets} shows the individual datasets. Uncleaned
C4.5 reaches 86.33\% accuracy and 81.92\% macro-F1. The two combined tree
methods reach 81.21\% and 80.89\% accuracy, losses of 5.12 and 5.44 pp.
Their macro-F1 scores also fall, to 75.76\% and 73.93\%. Adding attribute
selection after filtering recovers only 0.18 pp relative to Algorithm~1's
81.03\% average accuracy.

For NB, attribute weighting alone gives the highest mean in this comparison,
77.35\%, against 75.93\% for plain NB. The combined methods reach 75.63\%
and 76.35\%. Their changes relative to plain NB are $-0.30$ and $+0.42$ pp,
and neither improves on Algorithm~2's average. The reference arms help
locate the loss: filtering followed by NB reaches 75.42\%, while selecting
attributes before C4.5 leaves its mean almost unchanged at 86.19\%.

Averages conceal substantial dataset variation. On Glass, instances-first
NB falls from the plain NB baseline of 45.95\% to 30.93\%; instances-first
C4.5 falls from 66.58\% to 48.97\%. On Contact lenses, the same NB
combination improves from 73.00\% to 83.00\%, although that dataset has
only 24 rows. These differences warrant inspection at the dataset level
before describing a cleaner as helpful.

Table~\ref{tab:tests} reports the paired accuracy comparisons. AN--DT is
lower than the C4.5 baseline with raw $p=0.014$, but its Holm-adjusted
$p=0.109$ exceeds the stated threshold. None of the eight comparisons is
significant after adjustment. The observed mean losses remain relevant,
but this benchmark does not establish a general disadvantage or advantage
for every classification problem.

Changing from NA to AN raises mean NB accuracy by 0.71 pp and lowers mean
tree accuracy by 0.32 pp. The corresponding raw $p$ values are 0.275 and
0.922. We find no clear order advantage under these settings. This finding
is limited to a single pass through the two steps, with fixed learner
settings and the weights passed to the AN judge.

\subsection{Cleaning before the split changes the score}
Table~\ref{tab:results-protocol-a} compares the two evaluation protocols.
Algorithm~1 reaches 94.52\% after full-data filtering, against 81.03\%
when the judge is refitted within each training fold. The apparent gain is
13.50 pp. Algorithm~2 changes much less, from 77.35\% to 78.07\%, or
0.72 pp. The combined tree methods also rise sharply under Protocol A,
to 94.72\% and 96.32\%.

The procedures explain why these numbers answer different questions.
Protocol B asks the final classifier to predict all held-out cases.
Protocol A first uses the NB judge to choose which cases may reach the
evaluation. The large increase is therefore compatible with removing
cases the judge finds difficult, even when the downstream classifier
does not improve on the full population. Attribute-only cleaning retains
all rows, although its full-data selection still uses information from
future test folds. Its smaller score change here should not be read as
permission to select attributes before cross-validation.

\subsection{Deletion changes class representation}
The breast-cancer records show how uneven the removal can be. Across
Protocol B folds, the plain NB judge removes an average of 50.63\% of
recurrence-event rows and 14.51\% of no-recurrence-event rows. After
attribute selection, the weighted judge removes 58.97\% and 11.72\%,
respectively. These are mean class-specific removal rates, not percentages
of the entire dataset.

The minority class loses a much larger share of its training examples in
both paths. The records cannot establish whether those labels were wrong
or whether NB failed on difficult but valid cases. They do show why a
cleaner's effect needs to be assessed through both classification scores
and the training cases it discards.

\section{Limitations}
The benchmark consists of the original study's dataset family, with the
file differences described above. It does not establish performance on
modern datasets or on data with a known amount of label noise. In
particular, the NSL-KDD C4.5 baseline is 99.47\%, compared with 71.11\%
reported in 2014. That discrepancy remains unresolved. The current
comparison uses shared data and splits, but should not be described as an
exact replication of the earlier scores.

Rare classes limit the evaluation. Contact lenses has classes with four
and five rows, and NSL-KDD contains classes with fewer than ten examples,
including singleton classes. Some stratified folds therefore lack a class
in training or testing. Repeating the splits does not supply new examples,
and the fold-specific macro-F1 convention can average different class
sets. These results cannot support reliable rare-class performance claims.

Although model fitting and supervised cleaning are confined to training
rows in Protocol B, nominal category vocabularies come from the complete
files. The estimate is conditional on those known vocabularies; it does
not test deployment with unseen nominal values. No calibration or
out-of-fold judging is used to validate the filter's rejection decisions.
The tests involve ten datasets and can miss small effects, especially
after multiplicity adjustment. Finally, the Protocol A comparison cannot
identify which evaluation procedure the 2014 study used.

\section{Conclusion}
Algorithm~3 combines NB instance filtering and tree-based attribute
weighting in either order. On ten datasets under fold-specific evaluation,
both combined tree methods have lower mean accuracy and macro-F1 than
uncleaned C4.5, while both combined NB methods trail attribute weighting
alone. The paired tests provide no clear order advantage. The before-CV
scores and class-specific removal rates support a practical recommendation:
fit supervised cleaning within the training fold, score every held-out row,
and report which classes lose training cases.
